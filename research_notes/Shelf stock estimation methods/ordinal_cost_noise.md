# Ordinal, cost-sensitive learning under label noise with small data, plus evaluation pitfalls

Scope: 3-class ordinal stock level (empty < half < full; 81/15/4 %), asymmetric decision loss (correct +1, lower -2, higher -3), one human labeller with borderline half/full noise, small time-ordered data, 289-area hidden test set. Date of today's research: 2026-10-11. Source dates are given inline. Most searches returned abstracts or snippets, not full text; where a claim rests on that, it is flagged. Items marked "(my arithmetic)" are derived by me from the stated cost matrix, not from a source.

## Q1. Ordinal methods (cumulative link, Frank and Hall, regression + thresholds) vs multiclass probabilities + expected-value decision

### Takeaway
No source found benchmarks ordinal methods against "multiclass probabilities + EV decision" with gradient boosting, so the claim that ordinal methods win is unproven for this setup. Ordinal structure helps mainly by sharing statistical strength across classes (useful for the 4% empty class); the cost-sensitive decision step is what the cost matrix actually requires, and it can sit on top of either.

### Cited Findings
- Frank and Hall (ECML 2001) convert a K-class ordinal problem into K-1 binary problems ("is label > i?"), each trained independently with any base classifier, then merge outputs; later work criticises the independent training and the lack of an analysable combination step. Secondary descriptions only, original text not read. — [Frank and Hall abstract (mlanthology)](https://mlanthology.org/ecmlpkdd/2001/frank2001ecml-simple); [summary of critique in Data Replication related work](https://scitepress.org/Papers/2015/51916/pdf/index.html)
- Li and Lin (NeurIPS 2006), "Ordinal Regression by Extended Binary Classification": builds extended examples from the data plus a mislabeling cost matrix, trains any binary classifier, and builds a ranker from it; a weighted 0/1 loss of the binary classifier upper-bounds the ranker's mislabeling cost. The 2012 Neural Computation journal version ("Reduction from cost-sensitive ordinal ranking to weighted binary classification") adds error-wise and regret-wise bounds, and threshold ensembles are a special case. Abstract-level only. — [NeurIPS 2006 abstract](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2006/hash/019f8b946a256d9357eadc5ace2c8678-Abstract.html); [Lin PhD thesis, Caltech, threshold ensembles and AdaBoost extension](https://thesis.library.caltech.edu/2321/)
- Threshold-based ordinal models map a latent real score to K ordered labels with K-1 cut points; stock LightGBM/XGBoost objectives are not ordinal (their ranking objectives are pairwise/listwise), and a regressor-plus-thresholds design is awkward because the frameworks cannot optimise thresholds jointly with trees. — [OGBoost, arXiv 2502.13456 (Feb 2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13456); [ordinalgbt motivation page](https://ordinalgbt.readthedocs.io/en/latest/motivation.html)
- OGBoost (scikit-learn compatible) jointly optimises a latent regression function and a threshold vector; its abstract reports no numerical comparison against standard gradient boosting (only a UCI wine-quality illustration). — [OGBoost abstract (Feb 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13456)
- gbnet's GBOrd wraps XGBoost or LightGBM with an ordinal logistic loss (single score + learned thresholds). Documentation only, no benchmark seen. — [gbnet docs](https://gbnet.readthedocs.io/en/latest/autoapi/gbnet/models/ordinal_regression/index.html)
- Cumulative link models in deep nets (Vargas et al.) report improvement over a nominal model and over robust ordinal proposals; deep-learning context, not boosting. — [Cumulative link models for deep ordinal classification (2019 preprint)](https://astro.paperswithcode.com/paper/190513392)
- Cost-sensitive boosting for ordinal targets: Riccardi et al. (IEEE Trans. Cybernetics, 2014) extend SAMME to ordered targets with cost-sensitive weights and report competitive results (abstract-level). — [Riccardi et al. PDF](https://idus.us.es/bitstream/11441/133919/1/1-s2.0-S0020025521008896-main.pdf) (note: file name suggests a Information Sciences paper; venue and year attribution from the search summary not verified)
- THORS (arXiv 1811.02814, Nov 2018) picks an ordered set of m-1 thresholds minimising expected cost on a validation set for multiclass cost-sensitive problems. — [THORS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.02814)
- Kaggle practice: APTOS 2019 (graded 0-4, quadratic weighted kappa) top solutions commonly used a regression head with tuned thresholds. One public 10th-place repo reports regression CV 0.9225 vs classification 0.9213 (4-fold, SEResNext50), i.e. a near-tie, and LB 0.787 vs 0.772. Single solution, deep image models, QWK metric not an asymmetric cost. — [BloodAxe APTOS 2019 repo](https://github.com/BloodAxe/Kaggle-2019-Blindness-Detection)
- Elkan (IJCAI 2001): train on the data as given, then pick the class with lowest expected cost from the probabilities; rebalancing has little effect on Bayesian/tree learners. — [Elkan 2001](https://mlanthology.org/ijcai/2001/elkan2001ijcai-foundations)

### Inferences
- Decision structure (my arithmetic, from the project's scoring): EV(half) - EV(empty) = 3 p_h - 4 p_e, and EV(full) - EV(half) = 3 p_f - 4 p_h. So adjacent-class decisions are ratio tests: go up a level only if the higher class is more than 4/3 as probable as the lower one. When p_e is near 0, "full" beats "half" iff p_f / (p_f + p_h) > 4/7 = 0.571, not 0.5. Because the optimum depends only on adjacent ratios, a Frank-Hall style decomposition (P(y>=half), P(y>=full)) plus a tuned cut for each is a natural parameterisation, and a regression score with 2 tuned cutpoints is the cheapest version. Rows of this cost matrix are V-shaped, which is (to my recollection, not verified against the paper) the condition Li and Lin's reduction needs.
- Because the Bayes rule above only needs p_h/p_e and p_f/p_h, a well-calibrated multiclass model feeding the EV rule is already near-optimal; ordinal models should be judged by whether they give better-calibrated tail probabilities (p_e, p_h) with 4% and 15% priors, not by accuracy. A multiclass GBM with 4% empty (roughly 12 of 289 areas, and fewer in training frames) will have very noisy p_e; sharing information through an ordinal latent score or cumulative targets is the plausible mechanism for gain.
- Cheapest experiment under the no-new-dependency rule: (a) multiclass GBM + EV (baseline); (b) two Frank-Hall binary GBMs for P(y>=half), P(y>=full), converted to class probabilities by differencing, then EV; (c) one regressor on 0/1/2 with two cutpoints tuned for the cost matrix on out-of-fold predictions. All three use sklearn only. OGBoost/gbnet add a dependency and have no published advantage in what I found, so I would not add them without a DECISIONS.md entry.
- With flat per-direction costs (lower -2, higher -3 regardless of distance), tuned cutpoints on a regression score can be optimised directly by grid search on OOF predictions (2 parameters), which is far less variance-prone than tuning class weights.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head study of ordinal GBM vs multiclass GBM + cost-based decision on small tabular data. OGBoost paper's full PDF (not read) may contain comparisons.
- Original Frank and Hall and Li and Lin PDFs were not read in full; combination rule details and the V-shaped cost condition are not verified here.
- No quantitative source on how much ordinal methods help with a 4% extreme class specifically.

## Q2. Calibration for cost-sensitive decisions; thresholds tuned on out-of-fold predictions

### Takeaway
With under ~1000 calibration samples use low-parameter calibration (temperature or Platt/sigmoid), not isotonic. Tune any cutpoints on out-of-fold predictions, and treat the tuned thresholds as part of the model when evaluating (nested), otherwise the reported score is optimistic.

### Cited Findings
- scikit-learn docs: sigmoid is most effective for small samples; isotonic is more prone to overfitting on small datasets and only matches/beats sigmoid with enough data (roughly > 1000 samples). — [scikit-learn calibration guide (stable docs, version 1.9 era)](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)
- scikit-learn multiclass calibration via `CalibratedClassifierCV` is one-vs-rest per class then renormalised; the docs now also list `method="temperature"` (single parameter T on log-probabilities/logits, learned by minimising log loss on a hold-out set; does not change argmax). With `ensemble=False`, calibrator is trained on `cross_val_predict` outputs and one classifier is fit on all data. All classes must appear in every train and test split when `ensemble=True`, a real risk with 4% empty. — [scikit-learn calibration guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html)
- Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana (ICML 2005): boosted trees distort probabilities in a sigmoid shape; after Platt scaling or isotonic regression, boosted trees, random forests and SVMs give the best probabilities. — [Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana 2005](https://mlanthology.org/icml/2005/niculescumizil2005icml-predicting)
- Guo et al. (ICML 2017): temperature scaling, a single-parameter form of Platt scaling, is effective for modern neural nets. Neural-net context. — [Guo et al. 2017](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v70/guo17a.html)
- Dirichlet calibration (Kull et al., NeurIPS 2019): natively multiclass map equal to log-transform of probabilities, one linear layer, softmax; generalises beta calibration; reported gains in classwise-ECE, log-loss and Brier. Has O(K^2) parameters, so for K=3 that is 12 parameters. — [Kull et al. 2019 (arXiv 1910.12656)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.12656)
- Decision calibration (Zhao et al., arXiv 2107.05719, 2021): recalibrate so predicted and true class distributions are indistinguishable to a set of downstream decision-makers; reported improved decision loss on skin-lesion data; sample complexity polynomial in actions and classes. — [Zhao et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.05719)
- "Cost-Aware Calibration of Classifiers" (INFORMS Journal on Data Science, 2024) proposes a cost-aware calibration definition/metric and a Bayes-optimal MetaCal algorithm; this is distinct from ICML 2021 Meta-Cal (ranking-based, ECE-focused). Multiclass coverage not confirmed from snippets. — [Cost-Aware Calibration of Classifiers](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/full/10.1287/ijds.2024.0038); [Meta-Cal ICML 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.04290v2)
- sklearn `TunedThresholdClassifierCV` (docs 1.9.1): tunes the decision threshold for a cost/gain metric with 5-fold CV by default and refits afterwards. Tuning on training predictions (`cv="prefit"`, `refit=False` on the training data) gave an inflated-looking plateau the docs call symptomatic of overfitting; the example's cost-tuned model improved test gain from -209 to -143. Binary only. — [sklearn cost-sensitive example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_cost_sensitive_learning.html)
- Cawley and Talbot (JMLR 2010): over-fitting the model-selection criterion (including tuned hyperparameters) can cause performance loss comparable to the differences between algorithms; selection must be repeated inside each outer fold. — [Cawley and Talbot 2010](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html)

### Inferences
- Recommended calibration ladder for ~hundreds of labelled areas: temperature scaling (1 parameter) or per-class Platt on OOF probabilities first; Dirichlet (12 parameters) only if reliability diagrams show class-specific bias and a leave-frame-out check shows gain; skip isotonic.
- Since the decision is only sensitive to two ratios (p_f/p_h and p_h/p_e, see Q1), directly tuning one multiplicative bias per boundary on OOF predictions (equivalent to prior/odds shift) is a 2-parameter, decision-aligned calibration, much like Zhao et al.'s decision calibration but with minimal parameters. Evaluate it with nested/grouped CV; do not report the score on the same OOF predictions it was tuned on.
- `ensemble=True` calibration will break when a fold lacks an "empty" example; use `ensemble=False` or custom OOF calibration with grouped folds.
- Boosted tree probabilities are known to be sigmoid-distorted, so some calibration is warranted for the GBM stock source.

### Gaps
- No small-data (n < 500) multiclass ordinal calibration comparison found. No source quantifying typical decision-loss gains from cost-aware calibration over temperature scaling.
- Dirichlet vs temperature vs Platt performance at n ~ few hundred with 4% minority class: not found.
- Cost-Aware Calibration paper full text not read.

## Q3. Label noise handling (soft labels, confident learning, label smoothing, robust losses, relabelling, boundary modelling)

### Takeaway
Confident learning is directly usable with sklearn-style OOF probabilities but assumes class-conditional noise and is not ordinal-aware; for a single labeller whose noise is concentrated on the half/full boundary, ordinal soft labels or relabelling the flagged borderline cases are better matches. I found no source giving typical gains for small tabular data, so expectations should come from our own grouped CV.

### Cited Findings
- Confident Learning (Northcutt, Jiang, Chuang; arXiv Oct 2019, JAIR 2021): estimates the joint distribution of noisy vs true labels under a class-conditional noise assumption (noise depends on the true class, not the instance); prunes, counts with per-class thresholds, ranks; claims consistency under stated conditions and outperforming seven noisy-label methods on CIFAR. Fetched abstract says nothing about small data or ordinal labels. — [arXiv 1911.00068](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00068)
- cleanlab `find_label_issues` needs out-of-sample `pred_probs` (e.g. from cross-validation) and given labels, works with any classifier exposing `predict_proba`; `CleanLearning` wraps sklearn estimators; ranked indices available. No ordinal-specific handling found in the docs. — [cleanlab docs v2.x](https://docs.cleanlab.ai/v2.3.0/tutorials/indepth_overview.html)
- Soft labels for ordinal regression (Diaz and Marathe, CVPR 2019): turn each hard label into a soft distribution over ordered classes (mass decays with distance from the true label), usable with ordinary cross-entropy; competitive with specialised methods on age, quality, depth tasks. Deep-learning setting. — [Diaz and Marathe 2019](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_CVPR_2019/html/Diaz_Soft_Labels_for_Ordinal_Regression_CVPR_2019_paper.html)
- Robust Deep Ordinal Regression under Label Noise (Garg and Manwani, ACML 2020): noise-robust, rank-consistent versions of two ordinal losses under class-conditional noise; assumes the noise model is known. — [ACML 2020](https://mlanthology.org/acml/2020/garg2020acml-robust)
- Ordinal Adaptive Correction (ORDAC; arXiv 2509.02351, Sep 2025): corrects rather than discards noisy ordinal labels via per-sample label distributions; on Adience with 40% synthetic noise reports lower MAE and higher recall than baseline. Heavy synthetic noise, image data, deep model. — [arXiv 2509.02351](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.02351)
- Those ordinal-noise papers note adjacent classes are semantically close, so annotation is ambiguous and errors concentrate there. — [ORDAC](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02351v1)
- Generalized cross entropy (Zhang and Sabuncu, NeurIPS 2018) interpolates between MAE (noise-robust but hard to train) and CCE; tested on CIFAR/Fashion-MNIST with synthetic noise. Deep-net setting, not boosting. — [GCE](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/2018/hash/f2925f97bc13ad2852a7a551802feea0-Abstract.html)
- Survey of deep noisy-label methods (Song et al., TNNLS 2023; arXiv 2007.08199) categorises 62 methods; the retrieved snippets did not cover label smoothing effect sizes or small data. — [Song et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08199v7)

### Inferences
- Label smoothing and GCE are neural-net remedies; a sklearn GBM has no direct equivalent except (a) sample weights, (b) soft targets via duplicating rows with fractional weights (each borderline example appears as half and full with weights w and 1-w), or (c) regression on a soft ordinal target (e.g. fill-fraction). Option (b) or (c) is the practical ordinal-soft-label route for GBMs.
- Since noise here is boundary noise (half vs full) and asymmetric costs punish over-predicting more (-3 vs -2), noisy labels near the boundary push a model trained on hard labels toward over-confident "full"; soft/borderline weighting should lower p_f for ambiguous crops, which the EV rule then converts into "half" more often. This is reasoning, not a measured effect.
- Confident learning on 3 classes with class-conditional noise is a coarse tool; the useful output is the ranked list of suspected label issues from OOF probabilities, which a human can re-inspect (the labeller is one person, so relabelling a few dozen flagged borderline areas is cheap). It must not touch hidden validation images (project hard rule 1) and must use frame-grouped OOF predictions to avoid leakage (rule 6).
- Where labels carry a physical proxy (e.g. estimated fill percentage), modelling the annotator boundary directly (fit the full/half cutpoint on fill-fraction) is a stronger approach than generic noise-robust losses; I did not find a paper doing this for single-annotator boundary noise.

### Gaps
- No source with typical gains for confident learning, label smoothing or robust losses on small (hundreds of samples) tabular/embedding data.
- No source on annotator-specific boundary noise models; ORDAC and Garg and Manwani use class-conditional or distance-based noise instead.
- cleanlab ordinal support: not found.

## Q4. Conformal prediction, abstention, and gating calls to the VLM

### Takeaway
Conformal sets give a principled "non-singleton means defer" gate, but with exchangeability violated by time-ordered frames and very few empty/half examples, expect approximate coverage only. The simpler and more decision-aligned gate is the expected-regret (EV margin) of the current prediction, with the threshold chosen on grouped OOF data using the VLM's measured accuracy and cost.

### Cited Findings
- Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification" (arXiv 2107.07511, 2021; Foundations and Trends in ML, 2023): practitioner-oriented, includes Python/Jupyter examples, and covers structured outputs, distribution shift, time-series and models that abstain. Full text not parsed (PDF fetch failed), so formulas are not quoted. — [arXiv 2107.07511](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511v6); [author blog](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~angelopoulos/blog/posts/gentle-intro)
- Standard conformal for unordered classes often produces non-contiguous sets, a poor fit for ordinal labels; Dey, Merugu, Kaveri (NeurIPS 2023) adapt conformal methods to output contiguous sets with coverage guarantees using a unimodal non-parametric model. — [NeurIPS 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/029f699912bf3db747fe110948cc6169-Abstract.html)
- Haas et al. (UAI 2026, PMLR v337) use the ranked probability score as the nonconformity measure, giving median-centred contiguous sets by construction on ordinal image and tabular data. Abstract-level. — [Haas et al.](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v337/haas26a.html)
- "Conformalized Ordinal Classification with Marginal and Conditional Coverage" (arXiv 2404.16610, Apr 2024): contiguous sets from marginal conformal p-values, non-contiguous sets from class-conditional p-values, both with coverage proofs. — [arXiv 2404.16610](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16610v1)
- Selective classification background: Chow rejection and El-Yaniv and Wiener risk-coverage trade-off; Geifman and El-Yaniv use softmax response as the confidence score; Cortes et al. argue confidence-only rejection can fail when the predictor is not Bayes-optimal and propose joint predictor-rejector learning. Second-hand summary. — [search summary of selective prediction literature](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10125395)
- A 2026 preprint on medical VLMs frames deferral as a cost-sensitive trade-off: excessive deferral reduces utility, overly small prediction sets hide uncertainty. Only the title and snippet seen; arXiv ID in the result looks unusual, treat as unverified. — [arXiv 2607.28696](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.28696)

### Inferences
- Conformal on 3 classes: with class-conditional (Mondrian) calibration, the empty class has ~4% of calibration points. A finite-sample (n+1) quantile needs enough points per class (rule of thumb from conformal theory: at least about 1/alpha minus 1 per class for the correction to be non-vacuous, e.g. at alpha = 0.1 that is about 9 empty examples in the calibration fold; my recollection of the standard result, not confirmed from the fetched text). Marginal conformal can under-cover the rare class exactly where the cost is highest.
- Frames of one camera on one day are not exchangeable (temporal drift, same area repeated), so the guarantee holds only approximately; calibrate with grouped (by frame/camera) splits and report empirical coverage per class.
- The loss is asymmetric, so the right "uncertainty" is expected regret, not set size: regret = max_k EV(k) - EV(predicted), computed from calibrated probabilities. Defer to the VLM when regret exceeds the VLM's expected cost per call times its error-risk difference, i.e. call the VLM only where expected gain (reduction in expected loss) exceeds a fixed budget. Use the OOF rank of regret to pick the top-N share of areas that fits the API budget; sweep N and plot loss vs fraction deferred (a risk-coverage curve), choosing the knee on grouped OOF data. This is my recommendation, not a published recipe for this exact setting.
- A conformal singleton test (|set| = 1) is a reasonable secondary gate and its set is cheap to compute from the same OOF scores; with ordinal contiguity (RPS or the unimodal method) sets are interpretable ("{half, full}" means a boundary case). Use it as an agreement check against the regret gate rather than adding a library.

### Gaps
- Could not read the Angelopoulos and Bates PDF text, so no verified formulas for the (n+1)(1-alpha)/n quantile or class-conditional coverage.
- No published cascade study for "cheap classifier defers to VLM" under asymmetric ordinal costs found.
- Conformal under temporal dependence on small data: only referred to via the book's chapter list.

## Q5. Evaluation pitfalls for small, single-labeller, time-ordered data

### Takeaway
Random CV across frames of the same camera and area leaks; use grouped (by frame, and by camera for a stricter test) and forward-in-time validation, nest all tuning, and report cluster-bootstrap intervals. With 289 test areas the interval on mean score is wide enough that small model differences (a few tenths of a point per area or a handful of flipped areas) are not distinguishable without paired comparison.

### Cited Findings
- Roberts et al. (Ecography 2017): random CV on data with temporal, spatial or hierarchical dependence can seriously underestimate predictive error; block CV is "nearly universally more appropriate" for predicting to new data, but blocking can induce extrapolation by restricting predictor ranges. — [Roberts et al. 2017](https://pred.uni-regensburg.de/id/eprint/1455/); [PDF](https://www.biometrie.uni-freiburg.de/mitarbeiter/dormann/roberts-et-al-2017-ecography.pdf)
- Cawley and Talbot (JMLR 2010): selection bias from over-fitting the model-selection criterion can be as large as differences between algorithms; repeat selection inside each fold. — [Cawley and Talbot](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/cawley10a.html)
- Kapoor and Narayanan (Patterns 2023; arXiv Jul 2022): data leakage found in 17 fields affecting 294 papers (329 in the earlier arXiv version), taxonomy of eight leakage types; in the civil-war case study, correcting leakage removed the apparent advantage of ML over older methods. — [PMC version](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10499856); [arXiv 2207.07048](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2207.07048)
- sklearn: tuning a threshold on the training predictions looks overfit (wide plateau); tune on held-out folds and keep tuning data separate. — [sklearn cost-sensitive example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/model_selection/plot_cost_sensitive_learning.html)
- Standard bootstrap ignores within-cluster correlation and under-covers; cluster and hierarchical bootstrap (resample whole subjects) reach higher coverage when intra-cluster correlation exists. Shown for AUC with repeated measures. — [Iowa State repository paper on cluster bootstrap for AUC](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/entities/publication/715dec91-9bb1-4f5c-9b3a-ef24bfd2e1fc)
- Agreement as ceiling: a vendor glossary says a model cannot reliably exceed the accuracy implied by evaluation-label agreement (rule of thumb); Richie, Grover and Tsui (BioNLP 2022) dispute this, showing by simulation that ML can beat inter-annotator agreement even with noisy annotators if the model is well specified. Nothing found specific to a single annotator. — [claru.ai glossary](https://claru.ai/glossary/inter-annotator-agreement); [BioNLP 2022 paper](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2022.bionlp-1.26.pdf)
- "Label convergence" (arXiv 2409.09412) estimates an upper performance bound from contradictory annotations in object recognition, as a proxy for human-level performance. — [arXiv 2409.09412](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.09412)

### Inferences
- Leakage routes in this project: (1) same area, adjacent frames nearly identical, so random fold splits leak; (2) kNN and temporal features using the held-out frame's neighbours; (3) calibration and thresholds fitted on the same OOF predictions then scored on them; (4) VLM prompt or fusion weights tuned on the labelled frames; (5) label-cleaning (cleanlab, relabelling) done with models that saw the target frame. Controls: leave-one-frame-out as the outer loop, all fitting inside it, and a forward-in-time split (train on earlier frames, predict later ones) as a second, stricter estimate since the hidden set is probably later in the evening. The forward split will usually score lower; report both.
- Confidence intervals (my arithmetic): for a binary proportion with p = 0.90 and n = 289 areas, SE = sqrt(0.9 x 0.1 / 289) = 0.0176, so a 95% interval is about +/- 3.5 points, before accounting for clustering. For the mean per-area score (range -3 to +2, SD plausibly around 1), SE is about 1/sqrt(289) = 0.059 per point, i.e. +/- 0.12 on the 0-2 scale. Effective sample size is smaller because areas within an image, and the same area across frames, are correlated (26 images, 14 cameras). Use a cluster bootstrap resampling images (or cameras for a stricter version) and a paired bootstrap on the per-area score difference when comparing two models; report Wilson or exact intervals for class-specific rates. With roughly 12 empty and 43 half areas expected in 289, per-class recall has intervals tens of points wide (for 0 errors in 12, the rule-of-three upper bound is about 25%), so do not claim per-class improvements from the hidden set.
- Single-annotator ceiling: with one labeller, agreement with another labeller cannot be measured directly. Practical estimates: (a) have the same person relabel a random subset blind after a delay and compute weighted kappa or the actual score of their own first labels against the second pass (intra-annotator ceiling); (b) have a second person label ~50-100 borderline-rich areas and compute quadratic-weighted kappa. Per the BioNLP 2022 paper, the ceiling is soft: a good model can exceed measured agreement, but test scores near it cannot be read as model error alone.
- Hyperparameter and threshold search count as model selection (Cawley and Talbot): run the same search inside each outer fold, or hold out an untouched final fold set.
- Because the Sensei annotation may differ systematically from this labeller (a different half/full boundary), a boundary-shift sensitivity analysis (re-score after perturbing the labeller's boundary on borderline areas) is a cheap robustness check; no source for this, it is my suggestion.

### Gaps
- Did not find a source for the single-annotator intra-rater reliability ceiling or for a bootstrap recipe for per-area cost scores specifically; the Wilson/exact-interval and rule-of-three figures above are standard statistics stated without a citation retrieved this session.
- No published guidance on forward-in-time validation for fixed-camera retail imagery found; Roberts et al. (ecology) is the closest general source.
- Number of exchangeable clusters in the hidden set (26 images across up to 14 cameras) is from the project brief, not independently checked.
